Watch Tower history · Current study

Who chooses
the leaders?

From Russell’s congregational elections to the Society’s 1938 appointment system: how a belief about religious authority changed.

Published November 14, 2026 · Focus: 1904–1938 · Historical comparison, with one understanding check

The documented changeFact and interpretationSources and limitationsUnderstanding check

A new step in the investigation

This study moves from the early movement and succession disputes to a specific question: who could select the people exercising local religious authority? Earlier publications give us a way to compare the arrangements and their stated religious basis.

Academic context. Religious-studies scholar George D. Chryssides cautions against treating the later centralized organization as if it already existed in the same form at Russell’s death. His chapter also challenges overly simple accounts of the movement’s divisions. [4]

Three stages, not one sudden switch

Published arrangements for choosing local leaders
PeriodDocumented arrangementSource and what it establishes
Russell, 1904The New Creation assigns the election of elders to the congregation. Consecrated women and men could vote; this was not the same as allowing women to serve as representative elders.Study VI, especially pp. 279–282. Evidence of Russell’s published position, not proof that every congregation followed it. [1]
1932 transitionAnnual elections for elders and deacons were removed. Congregations still selected a service committee; its service director was appointed by the Society. Some voting therefore continued.Proclaimers, chapter 15, discussion of 1932 and footnote on the committee. A later institutional account identifying the August 15 and September 1, 1932 articles. [2]
1938 resolutionCongregations were invited to request Society appointment of their servants. Local members could recommend candidates; appointment authority lay with the Society.The Watchtower, June 15, 1938, “Organization” Part 2, paragraph 15 and resolution. Contemporaneous evidence of the policy being advocated. [3]

The crucial distinction: nominating a candidate and having the final authority to appoint that person are different powers.

Russell’s model had its own claims of authority

Russell encouraged believers to test teachings, including those coming through a recognized channel. He also allowed for a general channel of religious instruction and recommended his own Scripture Studies where a congregation lacked qualified elders. Local elections coexisted with his substantial teaching influence. [1]

It would therefore go beyond the evidence to describe Russell’s movement as having no influential religious authority, or to equate its elections with unrestricted participation by everyone attending.

A policy change and its religious justification

The 1938 article grounded Society appointments in its interpretation of Christ’s rule and the Society’s role as his earthly representative. That is a claim made by the publication. Historians can establish that the claim was published and used to justify the arrangement; publication itself cannot establish divine authorization. [3]

Documented: responsibility for selecting local leaders changed. Institutional interpretation: the Society’s later history presents the changes as a restoration of theocratic order. Historical inference: moving final appointment authority to the Society increased organizational centralization. This inference describes the distribution of authority; it does not establish a leader’s private motives. [2]

Claims that the change proved divine guidance, or that personal ambition fully explains it, require different kinds of argument. Neither conclusion follows simply from the appointment procedure.

One understanding check

Test the distinction.

Under the 1938 arrangement, what could the congregation recommend, and who held final appointment authority?

Show the explanation after answering

Local congregations could suggest suitable candidates. The Society made the appointments. A recommendation did not give the congregation the final decision. This documents the allocation of authority; it does not prove the claim of divine approval.
