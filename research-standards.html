Investigative methodology / Open inquiry

Every claim deserves a fair test. Not every claim has equal evidence.

Our Theory Lab welcomes academic, independent, private, and unconventional research. We label what is observed, what is inferred, and what remains unknown—without dismissing questions because they are unpopular.

Six complementary evidence labels

Primary records

Excavation notes, original photographs, instrument readings, public records and dated firsthand documentation. Record custody, provenance and limitations.

Peer-reviewed studies

Scholarly publications evaluated by other specialists. Peer review is a useful process, not a guarantee of correctness or consensus.

Independent or private research

Work outside traditional institutions, including privately funded fieldwork. Evaluate methods, access to raw data, conflicts of interest and reproducibility.

Interpretations and hypotheses

Proposed explanations, whether conventional or unconventional. Require explicit predictions and separate supporting observations from assumptions.

Disputed or unsupported claims

Assertions with contradictory findings, unclear provenance or inadequate testing. Preserve a fair description and identify what could change the assessment.

Unresolved questions

Gaps in measurement, dating, access or interpretation. An unknown is not automatically evidence for any specific explanation.

These labels are not mutually exclusive: a controversial idea may be peer-reviewed; a private expedition may produce valuable primary data.

The five-question evidence check

  1. What exactly is claimed? State it narrowly enough to be falsifiable or independently checked.
  2. Where did it originate? Find the first report, document, dataset, observation or attributed interview.
  3. What supports and contradicts it? Include the strongest available counterevidence, not only favorable sources.
  4. What would change our minds? Specify a measurement, independent replication, excavation or newly released document.
  5. What remains unknown? Mark missing information and avoid implying certainty from silence.

How comparisons are presented

FieldMeaning
ObservationWhat a cited source actually recorded or measured.
ExplanationWhat a researcher proposes the observation means.
Evidence for / againstIndependent support, conflicting observations and alternative causes.
ConfidenceProvisional assessment based on evidence quality, not popularity.
Verification pathTest or document needed to strengthen or weaken the explanation.
Source and fundingPublisher, author, date, study type and disclosed funding where known.
Editorial safeguard: A citation establishes what a source says, not necessarily that its conclusion is true. Correlation does not establish causation; association does not establish misconduct. We correct records when stronger evidence emerges.
